\begin{abstract}
Long-running agents accumulate facts that change, expire, and must sometimes be
deleted. Answering \emph{what was true then}, and \emph{what the agent knew then},
requires separating valid time from transaction time and honouring deletion
across every store a query can reach. Agent memory systems now advertise these
capabilities; we measure whether they deliver them.

We replay deterministic lifecycle histories, scored against an independently
cross-checked oracle, into five open-source systems---Mem0, LangMem, Graphiti, Cognee and
Hindsight---under forward-checkpoint transaction-time replay. Two designs emerge.
\emph{Consolidating} stores overwrite superseded values, so they answer current
queries well (0.952 and 0.890) but historical ones poorly (0.364 and 0.323),
just under a ceiling (0.385) that the history alone fixes, and Mem0 applies a
correction about the past to the present (1 of 45 current values answered
afterwards).
\emph{Retaining} stores keep the history but do not filter it: read at the
retrieval layer, three independent implementations score 0.000 on valid-time
queries, yet one deterministic read-path rule recovers up to 190 of 195 from
the dates they return. An LLM reader recovers it for Cognee (192 of 195) but
not for Hindsight (67). Mem0's current release switches to the second design, and
its accuracy follows. In Mem0 and Graphiti, deletion
divides the same way: native deletion APIs clear retrieval-reachable stores,
while deletion requested in prose is silently ignored. Both failure modes hold
from 8 to 128 people per history and on 62 supersessions in LongMemEval's
annotator-edited conversations. Cost is write-dominated in Mem0 and
read-dominated in Cognee: where it falls is a design choice.

We claim no novelty for bitemporal modelling or forgetting evaluation: the
contribution is measurement, and the finding that a system can expose temporal
fields without answering temporal queries correctly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An agent that remembers must also revise, expire, and forget. Two questions
recur: \emph{what was true at time $t$} (valid time) and \emph{what did the
system know at time $t$} (transaction time). Current-state stores conflate them,
and deletion is harder still---a value removed from a serving index may survive
in a history table, a vector store, or a raw episode log, ready to resurface.

These needs are recognised. Graphiti attaches creation, validity, invalidity and
expiry timestamps to graph edges; Mem0 records add/update/delete history beside
a mutable vector store; Hindsight combines invalidation archives, histories,
audit logs and derived-record cleanup; and recent work makes the temporal model
explicit, from a graph-native bitemporal store to a bitemporal operator algebra
and a bi-temporal ledger with deterministic supersession.

Given that, the open question is not \emph{whether to model time} but
\textbf{whether the modelling produces correct answers under the operations
agents actually perform}. We answer it by measurement:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a deterministic lifecycle generator whose ground truth is computed by an
        oracle and checked by a second implementation of it;
  \item a natural-language rendering of those histories so opaque systems can be
        scored against the same truth;
  \item forward-checkpoint transaction-time replay, so an \texttt{as-of} query is
        answered from a store that has seen only what was known by then;
  \item three readings of every answer --- as returned, by one common reader,
        and by the value it asserts --- so that what a store holds is separated
        from how its answers are worded;
  \item a deletion protocol that compares prose requests with each system's
        deletion API, called both with the right handles and as a caller
        without them would, and scans every store for what survives.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) An identical-history comparison of five deployed memory systems, separating
consolidating from retaining designs; each side of the taxonomy is confirmed on
implementations held out from its construction, and both failure modes reappear
on 62 supersessions in LongMemEval's annotator-edited conversations
(\S\ref{sec:realdata}). A
ceiling computable from the history alone caps the consolidating stores
(\S\ref{sec:bounds}), and a retroactive correction, tested against predictions
committed before the runs, overwrites Mem0's present (\S\ref{sec:corrections}).
On the current releases the picture holds, except that Mem0 has switched
designs and its accuracy has moved with it, and Hindsight's reader now restates
the superseded value beside the right one (\S\ref{sec:releases}).
(ii) A separation of \emph{information loss} from \emph{missing filtering}:
the retaining stores answer no valid-time question at the retrieval layer, but
read by one common reader, or by their own readers credited for the value they
assert, they answer 0.73 to 1.00, while the consolidating stores stay under
their ceiling (\S\ref{sec:reader}). A gap we had reported between two readers
was one of answer form. On benchmark text a plain reader recovers almost none of
it, because it does not assume a value persists until revised.
(iii) A cross-store deletion-completeness measurement under both a prose
instruction and each system's native API, which the systems describe as a
feature but do not measure, and which prior deletion benchmarks judge by what a
reader recovers rather than by what the stores retain.
(iv) Measured write/read cost and latency, and a reproducible sealed harness
whose every reported number recomputes offline from per-case transcripts,
including preserved negative results and four retracted measurements.
Code, data, sealed runs, and the scripts that recompute every reported number
from them are public at \url{https://github.com/suanlab/temvera}.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Both questions are database questions first.}
Valid time and transaction time are the two axes of temporal databases
\citep{snodgrass1985,jensen1999temporal}; TSQL2 \citep{snodgrass1995tsql2} and
then SQL:2011's application-time and system-versioned tables
\citep{kulkarni2012sql2011} standardised them, and TPC-BiH
\citep{kaufmann2013tpcbih} benchmarks time-travel queries on both axes over
relational engines. We take the semantics and the benchmarking stance from this
work: our oracle implements the same two axes, and our questions are the
agent-memory analogue of time-travel queries. Deletion has a database history
too. GDPR's right to erasure has been turned into database requirements and
benchmarked \citep{shastri2020gdpr}, argued for as a property to build in rather
than retrofit \citep{schwarzkopf2019gdpr}, and traced down to storage, where a
delete is a tombstone with no bound on when the data is gone
\citep{sarkar2020lethe}. Agent memory adds copies no database delete path knows
about --- extracted facts, LLM-written summaries, request logs --- and our
residue scan asks the same question of them.

\paragraph{Temporal modelling for agent memory is prior art.}
A graph-native bitemporal memory store \citep{graphnativebitemporal} keeps valid- and transaction-time
intervals with point-in-time retrieval, and TOKI \citep{toki} defines bitemporal operators
over a dual-row schema. MemStrata \citep{memstrata} pairs a bi-temporal ledger
with a deterministic (subject, relation, object) supersession rule and reports
stale-fact rate as its headline metric, and MemTX \citep{memtx} and MemTxn
\citep{memtxn} add transactional commit and recovery boundaries to agent memory.
We therefore claim none of these.

\paragraph{Forgetting evaluation is prior art.}
ForgetEval \citep{forgetevalpaper,forgeteval} generates seeded lifecycle cases
across supersession, decay, amnesia, purge and drift, calls each system's native
delete, and passes a case when no forbidden string reaches the top ten
retrieved; MemoryAgentBench \citep{memoryagentbench} evaluates selective
forgetting among four competencies. Springdrift \citep{springdrift} already
combines append-only memory, auditability and git-backed recovery. Bemula
\citep{bemula} sends forget requests in dialogue to six systems, scores leaks and
over-deletion from answers, and finds that none passes more than 1.5\% end to
end; MemCut \citep{memcut} recovers deleted secrets from objects that survive
its own deletion harness, and MemLeak \citep{memleak} from retained images and
correlated text. Read in full, none of them compares a prose request with the
same systems' delete calls, and none reads the systems' stores: ForgetEval and
Bemula judge deletion from what retrieval or an answer reveals.

\paragraph{Measuring deployed memory.}
Zhou et al.\ \citep{agentnative} evaluate twelve memory systems on eleven
datasets from a data-management perspective, including update robustness on
LongMemEval's knowledge-update slice, cost and component ablations, and Wolff
and Bennati \citep{wolffcost} compare the accuracy and cost of Mem0, Graphiti and
Cognee. STALE \citep{stale} and StateMemBench \citep{statemembench} find that
memory systems often answer with a superseded state, Supersede \citep{supersede}
isolates that gap on LongMemEval's knowledge-update questions, and Ground Truth
First \citep{groundtruthfirst} asks as-of-date questions over facts with
validity intervals on its own backends, at replay checkpoints where valid and
transaction time coincide. These score the current state or valid time; none we
found asks what a deployed system \emph{knew} at an earlier time, or replays one
seeded history into every system so that a difference is the system's
(Table~\ref{tab:related}). They go further than we do elsewhere: Zhou et al.\ in
systems, data and cost, Bemula in forms of forgetting, attacks and backbones,
ForgetEval in failure categories.

\paragraph{What is left.}
No prior work we found measures, under one controlled history, both bitemporal
answer correctness \emph{and} where deleted content physically survives across a
system's derived stores; the works above each measure one side, on other
systems or by other means. Our literature review is single-reviewer with a
deterministic 10\% rescreen; searches, exclusions and rescreens are logged and
frozen with the artifact, the last search on 19 October 2026, and the seven
closest works were re-read in full, with their released code, on 23 October.

\section{Which fragment of bitemporal semantics does each system implement?}
\label{sec:fragment}

The query we need is standard \citep{snodgrass1985}: for subject $s$ and
attribute $a$, return the value valid at event time $V$ according to what the
store had recorded by transaction time $T$. Answering it requires six
capabilities, and the systems differ less in whether they \emph{have} temporal
metadata than in what can be \emph{predicated} on it. We therefore classify
support into five levels, which account for what we measure in
\S\ref{sec:results}:

\begin{description}[\IEEEsetlabelwidth{\textsc{mismatch}}]
  \item[\textsc{absent}] the information is not stored;
  \item[\textsc{prose}] stored, but only inside free text, so no predicate can
        filter on it;
  \item[\textsc{off}] a queryable field exists and a filter is available, but is
        not applied by the default retrieval path;
  \item[\textsc{mismatch}] a queryable field exists and is applied, but denotes a
        different timeline than the query requires;
  \item[\textsc{yes}] representable, queryable, and applied.
\end{description}

Three entries in Table~\ref{tab:fragment} carry most of the explanatory weight.

\textbf{C2 \textsc{mismatch} is a second timeline, not a bug.} Two recording
times must be told apart. The \emph{physical} one is when the store wrote a
fact; the \emph{logical} one is when the agent learned it --- the date of the
conversation turn that stated it. In live use they coincide. They part whenever
a history is imported, backfilled, replayed or ingested late, and an agent's
``what did I know on 18 February'' asks about the logical one. Graphiti's
\texttt{created\_at} and \texttt{expired\_at} are a correct physical
transaction time: in our runs they carry 2026 timestamps for turns dated 2025.
Graphiti has no field for the logical time, so no predicate over its store can
express ``as of what was known on 2025-02-18'' for any history not ingested
live. This is why we ask transaction-time questions in two ways: at each
checkpoint of a forward replay, which every store can answer by construction,
and of the final store after the whole history (\S\ref{sec:corrections}), which
needs the predicate none of them has --- and why C5 is \textsc{absent} rather
than \textsc{off}.

\textbf{C1 \textsc{prose} is the distinction a capability checklist misses.}
LangMem does retain validity information --- its stored memories contain strings
such as ``valid from January 3, 2025''. But the store holds opaque values under
a namespace, so validity is legible to a reader and invisible to a query
planner. A survey that asked ``does the system record when a fact became true''
would score LangMem as supporting valid time; no predicate can use it.

\textbf{C3 separates closing an interval from overwriting a row.} Mem0 and
LangMem consolidate: a superseding fact replaces its predecessor, so the prior
value is destroyed rather than bounded. Graphiti closes intervals by setting
\texttt{invalid\_at}, but does so unreliably: direct inspection of the edge
metadata found supersessions that leave the field unset, and dated
invalidations that are self-contradictory ($\texttt{invalid\_at} \le
\texttt{valid\_at}$). We give no counts because that inspection ran against a
graph later retracted for cross-run contamination
(\S\ref{sec:negative}). C3 is therefore the axis on
which the two design families separate, and \S\ref{sec:results} measures the
consequence: consolidating stores lose history outright, while Graphiti retains
it and fails to filter it.

The reader should note where the causal chain terminates. C2 and C1 are
data-model properties. C3's unreliability in Graphiti is not: it is a property of
the LLM extraction step that populates the store; a stronger extractor did not
change the accuracy it leads to (\S\ref{sec:results}), though we did not
re-inspect the stored fields under it. For LLM-backed temporal stores the
extraction layer, not the schema, decides what the temporal fields hold --- a
statement about the pipeline that fills a temporal database, and the reason a
schema-level capability audit would mis-rank every system in
Table~\ref{tab:fragment}. That table covers the three systems whose stores we
inspected directly; Cognee and Hindsight enter in \S\ref{sec:reader} and are
characterised by measured behaviour rather than by schema inspection.

The ordering inverts. The two systems that advertise temporal capability are the
two whose retrieval answered no valid-time query at all, while the two that
advertise none --- one of which, LangMem, describes its own manager as
consolidating, and so tells the reader plainly that history is not kept --- score
highest. We do not read this as five systems' worth of evidence for a general
law, and the mechanism in each case is the one \S\ref{sec:results} measures
rather than anything about marketing. What it does establish is that
documentation is not a usable proxy here: on this sample, reading the project's
own description of its temporal support would have ranked the systems
\emph{backwards}. That is the practical form of the paper's claim --- a system
can expose temporal fields, document them accurately, and still not answer a
temporal query --- and it is why we measure rather than survey.

Two honest limits. Mem0 and LangMem are not failing a promise they made, so
their low valid-time is a design consequence and not a discrepancy; the
discrepancy rows are Graphiti and Hindsight. And Cognee's documented
\texttt{forget} is the one claim here our residual protocol never exercised,
since that experiment covered Mem0 and Graphiti only; \S\ref{sec:results}
examines its API surface instead, which is weaker evidence than a measurement
and is reported as such.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Workload.} A seeded generator emits typed histories over
entity/attribute state machines with configurable reconfirm, expire and purge
probabilities. Query truth is computed by the oracle independently of retrieval.

\paragraph{Why synthetic histories first.} Exact answers on both time axes need
a history in which every valid interval and every recording time is known, and
whose mix of revision, expiry and purge can be varied; no public conversational
benchmark labels more than the current value. Synthetic histories give that
control, and a public benchmark's conversations check it (\S\ref{sec:realdata}).
The price is realism, which is why that check is read as direction, not as
rates.

\paragraph{Rendering.} Histories are rendered to dated conversational turns.
The generator's synthetic tokens (\texttt{entity-000}, \texttt{place-38})
collapse under a semantic embedder, leaving systems unable to tell entities
apart, so an injective relabelling maps them to distinct natural names. The
relabelling preserves oracle semantics exactly and doubled Mem0's measured exact
accuracy, isolating tokenisation from temporal semantics.

\paragraph{Replay.} Transaction time is monotonic, so turns are ingested in
recorded order and every query is answered at the checkpoint matching its
transaction time. This costs $O(\text{turns})$ per cell and, unlike single-pass
ingestion, actually tests whether a store can answer as of a past point.

\paragraph{Scoring.} Deterministic. Let $E$ be the expected values at the queried
$(\text{valid time}, \text{transaction time})$ and $S$ the superseded values for
the same subject/attribute. A response is \emph{exact} iff every $e \in E$ occurs
in the returned text and no $s \in S$ does; when $E = \emptyset$ (the fact was
purged or expired) exactness requires that no $s \in S$ occurs, i.e. correct
abstention. Evidence recall is $|E \cap \text{returned}| / |E|$, and stale-use is
the fraction of returned values drawn from $S$. \textbf{Because exactness
quantifies over the whole returned set, it is a function of the retrieval budget
$k$}; we set $k{=}5$ throughout and report a sweep over $k$ in
Figure~\ref{fig:budget}. Rates are case-weighted with Wilson 95\% intervals, and
abstention is computed only over cells that contain abstention cases. Two
comparisons are cell-averaged instead, because they aggregate cells whose
denominators differ --- the internal baselines of Table~\ref{tab:baselines} and
the filtered-Graphiti runs --- and both are marked where they are used.
Cases share the histories they are asked of, so we also resample whole cells
(4,000 cluster-bootstrap draws); the comparisons the conclusions rest on are
paired on identical cases and tested with exact McNemar tests, Holm-adjusted over
a family of six declared in the artifact, and every adjusted $p$ is below
$10^{-18}$. The one claim of \emph{no} difference --- Mem0 against Graphiti at
$k{=}1$ --- passes an equivalence test with margin 0.10 (difference $+0.026$,
90\% cluster interval $[0.000, 0.057]$).

\paragraph{The oracle defines ground truth.} Expected values come from our
reference implementation, a typed append-only event log (\texttt{ingest},
\texttt{reconfirm}, \texttt{supersede}, \texttt{expire}, \texttt{purge}) folded
into beliefs that carry a valid-time interval and a transaction time. A second
SQLite implementation of the same specification checks that fold; the two agree,
which is a differential test of our own code, not external validation. The
reference implementation is not a system under test: it scores $1.0$ by
construction, appears only as a scale anchor and as the reference column of the
deletion scan, and its other machinery plays no part in the measurements.

\paragraph{Systems.} Internal baselines (append-only, full/recent context, BM25,
last-write-wins) and the oracle, and five external systems run at their
libraries' defaults, as a user would deploy them (Table~\ref{tab:settings}).
All use \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} (\texttt{gpt-4o} only where \S\ref{sec:results}
says so). The Hindsight runs did not record their version; the interpreter that
served them had \texttt{hindsight-api 0.9.1} installed throughout. LangMem and
Cognee require \texttt{openai} major versions incompatible with the pinned Mem0
and Graphiti, so they run in isolated interpreters behind a stdio protocol. Runs
are sealed with canonical config, environment, source hash and per-file
checksums.

\paragraph{Versions.} These systems change quickly, and our claims are about the
versions named above, measured between July and October 2026. Every sealed run
records the environment it ran under, and re-running a newer release is one
command per system, and we did so on the releases current on 22 October 2026
(\S\ref{sec:releases}). What we conclude concerns design --- whether a store
overwrites or retains, and where filtering happens. Three systems' current
retrieval layers behave as the measured ones; Mem0's changed its design, and its
accuracy changed exactly as the design predicts. The one implementation defect
we report (\S\ref{sec:external}) is marked as such.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The oracle is exact; non-temporal baselines are not}

Across 27 conditions (3 profiles $\times$ 3 scales $\times$ 3 revision counts)
with 10 seeds each --- 270 oracle cells --- the oracle held exact/recall/stale at
$1/1/0$ throughout, confirming the natural-language rendering and the scorer
round-trip the intended semantics without loss (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). Append-only and full context retrieve the
evidence ($0.915$ recall) but cannot choose among versions ($0.272$ exact).
Under the high-churn profile append-only falls to $0.255$ exact with $0.662$
stale. Two of these strawmen round to the same three digits as external systems
in Table~\ref{tab:external} --- last-write-wins and Mem0 at $0.603$, BM25@1 and
LangMem at $0.576$ --- which is coincidence: they are different measurements
over different workloads and diverge in the fourth decimal.

nearly flat (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}). We therefore do not claim Graphiti cannot represent
valid time. The defensible claim is narrower and does not depend on $k$: its
retrieval does not apply the validity metadata it stores, so downstream filtering
is left to the caller.

result: Graphiti under its shipped hybrid retrieval scores $0.196$ against
$0.194$ cosine-only with valid-time $0.000$ either way, and Mem0 with an
isolated database scores $0.603$ against $0.612$. These reruns were also our
first same-protocol replication, and it is instructive: Mem0's expiry accuracy
moved $0.000 \to 0.125$ (2 of 16 cases) between runs, exactly the instability its
Wilson interval predicts. We therefore draw no conclusion from either system's
expiry number.

\textbf{A stronger backbone moves no system across the divide.} Re-running the
whole 20-cell grid with \texttt{gpt-4o} in place of \texttt{gpt-4o-mini},
otherwise unchanged, moves no category of Mem0 or Graphiti by more than 2 of its
cases. Mem0 goes from $0.603$ to $0.607$ overall and from $0.364$ to $0.374$ on
valid time; Graphiti from $0.196$ to $0.194$ overall, and on valid time it
answers 0 of 195 under both. The other three move more but stay on their side:
LangMem's valid-time accuracy falls from 0.323 to 0.262, still under the ceiling
of \S\ref{sec:bounds} with no answer above it, and its transaction-scoped
accuracy from 0.890 to 0.789, below the 0.80 we had predicted; Hindsight's
retrieval layer answers 0 of 195 valid-time questions under both; Cognee's
reader answers 194, Hindsight's 159 exactly where it answered 67. The exact gap
between the two readers therefore depends on how the model words its answers,
which \S\ref{sec:reader} shows directly; the design classes do not.

\textbf{Repeated runs agree, except one reader.} Three further runs of every
configuration on four of the grid's cells (97 cases), otherwise identical,
reproduce the cited overall accuracy exactly for Mem0 and Graphiti (60 and 20)
and for the retrieval layers of Cognee and Hindsight (20), move LangMem and
Cognee's reader by at most 2 cases, and leave every retrieval layer at 0 of 38
valid-time questions. Hindsight's reader, which reads at temperature 0.9, moves
by up to 10 cases and answers 8, 12 and 14 valid-time questions where the cited
run answered 19, though still fewer than Cognee's reader (38) in every run.
Run-to-run variation is below the effects we report everywhere except in that
reader's exact score, which we therefore do not read as a stable number.

\textbf{Does Graphiti's own filter repair it?} Enabling its native valid-time
\texttt{SearchFilter} raises exact from $0.224$ to $0.318$ and cuts stale-use
from $0.561$ to $0.360$, but collapses evidence recall from $0.873$ to $0.318$:
precision is bought by discarding evidence, and valid-time remains $0.093$.
(Rates in this paragraph are cell-averaged, matching how the filtered runs were
scored; the case-weighted valid-time is $0.114$.)
Two structural limits cap it. First, the filter's predicate is only as good as
the extracted \texttt{invalid\_at} it reads: where supersession leaves that
field unset, a stale edge satisfies \texttt{invalid\_at IS NULL} at every event
time and is returned regardless. We inspected the edge metadata directly and
found both unset and self-contradictory
($\texttt{invalid\_at} \le \texttt{valid\_at}$) invalidations, but that
inspection ran against a graph later retracted for cross-run contamination, so
we report the mechanism and withhold its counts rather than cite numbers drawn
from a mixed ingestion. Second,
\texttt{created\_at}/\texttt{expired\_at} record wall-clock ingestion rather than
the history's transaction time, so no \texttt{as-of} bound is expressible. This
ceiling is not an extraction-quality artifact: with \texttt{gpt-4o} in place of
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} on four matched cells, valid-time is unchanged
($0.111 \to 0.111$) and expiry stays $0.000$, though \texttt{transaction\_as\_of}
improves ($0.514 \to 0.625$) and purge degrades ($0.296 \to 0.176$); with two
seeds these movements are within noise and we draw no conclusion from them.

\subsection{Deletion: the API works, the instruction does not}

Our first attempt at this measurement was unfair in two ways, and correcting it
reversed the conclusion. It gave our substrate a typed \texttt{purge} operation
while giving the deployed systems an English sentence, and that sentence
restated the value it asked to delete --- so any system storing its input
verbatim was penalised by construction. We now run two conditions with a
non-restating instruction (Figure~\ref{fig:residual}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/residual.pdf}

Under a natural-language deletion instruction both deployed systems retain the value in reachable stores; under each system's native deletion API the reachable stores fall to zero and only audit logs and entity summaries retain it.}
\caption{Where a purged payload survives, split by whether a query path can

\label{fig:residual}

Under a \textbf{natural-language instruction}, residual occurrences of the
purged value are the reference implementation 3 (audit ledger only), Mem0 15 (exposed memories 3,
vector store 3, history 9), Graphiti 18 (edges 3, entity nodes 9, episodes 6).
Under each system's \textbf{native deletion API} (\texttt{Memory.delete},
\texttt{remove\_episode}) the picture changes completely: Mem0 falls to 12 with
\emph{zero} in exposed memories and \emph{zero} in the vector store, and
Graphiti falls to 3 with \emph{zero} in edges and episodes.

Two conclusions follow, and the first contradicts what we previously reported.
\textbf{Both systems' deletion APIs do clear their retrieval-reachable stores};
purged content does not linger in the serving path when deletion is actually
invoked. What fails is \textbf{deletion expressed in prose}: told in natural
language to delete a fact, neither system acts on it, and the value remains
retrievable. For agents that receive deletion requests conversationally --- the
normal case for a user asking an assistant to forget something --- that gap is
the operationally relevant one.

Two residues survive API deletion. Mem0's SQLite history retains 12 occurrences
and in fact \emph{grows}, because deletion appends history rows; Graphiti's
LLM-generated entity summaries retain 3, which \texttt{remove\_episode} does not
rewrite. Mem0's history and our own append-only ledger are the same class of
store --- an audit log no query path reads --- so we do not claim our substrate
is uniquely clean. Counts are substring occurrences over differently shaped
stores and are not comparable in magnitude; the reachability split is the claim.
This rests on a single seeded history (12 events, three purged values) and one
backbone, with extraction that is not bit-reproducible.

\textbf{Why the instruction has nothing to bind to.} The measurement above says
prose deletion is ignored; reading the deletion surface of each library says why.
We inspected the installed distributions rather than their documentation:

what is needed and leaves the predicate to the caller. It also settles the case
\S\ref{sec:claims} left open. Cognee documents \texttt{forget} as one of four
core operations, and \texttt{forget} is real --- but it takes a
\texttt{data\_id}, a dataset, or \texttt{everything}, never a condition, so the
documented operation does not answer the documented use. We did not run Cognee

not a measurement of what survives in its stores.

fixed and preregistered before it was run, separate what a store holds from how
its answers are worded. A common reader --- one prompt at temperature 0 over

73 and 71 of 195 from Mem0's and LangMem's top five, under the 0.385 ceiling of

LangMem's five are memories that keep the previous value in their text
(``changed from Lima to Cairo'') --- a partial retention we had predicted it would
not show. \textbf{Retention decides whether correctness is recoverable at all;
the retrieval layer of every retaining system we measured leaves the recovery to
the caller.} A deployment on a consolidating store cannot be made correct
without changing the store; a deployment on a retaining store can be, on the
read path.

\textbf{The retention policy alone reproduces the split.} The deployed systems
differ in extractor, retriever and reader as well as in what they keep. To
isolate the policy we built two stores from the same rendered turns --- one
keeping every turn, one keeping only the statement of each person's latest
value --- and searched and read both identically (BM25, top five, the common
reader); nothing else differs. Consolidating, the store answers 75 of 195
valid-time questions as returned and 74 read, against a ceiling of 75 and with
none beyond it; retaining, it answers 31 as returned and 151 read. Read, both
answer all 228 transaction-scoped questions. The retaining store's 31 is not 0,
as for the deployed retrievers, because BM25 sometimes leaves the older statement
out of the top five; we had predicted at most 0.05.

\textbf{One rule on the read path is enough here.} The retaining stores return
the dates along with the values: Cognee's chunks are the stored turns, and
Hindsight restates each fact with its validity. A read path that keeps only the
dated statements about the queried subject and answers with the latest one not
after the question's valid time --- one rule, fixed and committed before it was
run, using no LLM and no knowledge of the answer --- lifts valid-time accuracy
from 0 to 184 of 195 for Cognee and to 190 of 195 for Hindsight; the misses are
statements the rule's pattern over-captures. Graphiti's facts carry no dates in
their text, so the rule applies to only 17 of its 195 answers and recovers 6.
The rule matches our rendering's sentence pattern, and Cognee's dates are the
ones our rendering wrote, so it shows what the returned text supports, not a
general-purpose filter; on benchmark text (\S\ref{sec:realdata}) we use the
common reader instead.

answer, so their exact columns show what one reader achieves, not a bound on what
a better one could, and not a like-for-like win over the retrieval-layer rows. Expiry stays low
for both readers ($0.125$ at best over 16 cases), so we draw no conclusion there.
The \texttt{purge} column is scored as our generator specifies it, which removes

attribute; under the request's own scope every configuration answers 0 or 1 of
45 except LangMem (9), and Cognee's reader earns its $0.622$ mostly by answering
a value it was asked to delete.

asynchronous client was not covered by it in these runs.

with Table~\ref{tab:external}: it is the first multi-attribute configuration
(three attributes per entity, each drawn from a different semantic class) and
therefore a different workload. Case-weighted exact accuracy moved
$0.609 \to 0.527 \to 0.539$ as entities grew $2 \to 4 \to 8$, which is not monotone and rests on three seeds
per scale. The 8-entity point is also not like the other two: its histories
draw more distinct values than the natural-name relabelling holds, and 19\% of
them fell back to synthetic tokens of the kind the relabelling exists to avoid
(none did at 2 or 4 entities). We draw nothing from the trend.

\subsection{Does it hold beyond our generator?}\label{sec:realdata}

The grid controls the history; it cannot show that the failure modes survive
text we did not generate. LongMemEval's knowledge-update questions
\citep{longmemeval} supply that case, in sessions an LLM simulated by self-chat
and annotators then edited: a user states a fact in one session and
revises it in a later one, each session timestamped. The benchmark ships the
current value as gold but never the superseded one, so we recovered both. Two
extractors (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and \texttt{gpt-4o}) each proposed the
attribute and its earlier and later value for all 69 non-abstention instances,
and a value counted only if it appeared verbatim in its own session. They
agreed on 43 pairs. The other 26 we adjudicated: the AI system proposed a label
or an exclusion for each, quoting the session text, and the author decided,
labelling 19 and excluding 7 whose sessions state no superseded value, state
two, or describe coexisting items rather than a revision. That leaves 62 of the
69 pairs. The 19 adjudicated pairs ran after the 43, against directional
predictions committed beforehand, which held; every comparison below points
the same way on either subset.

Each pair is replayed into a fresh store, session by session in recorded order.
After the earlier session we ask ``What is my \emph{attribute}?''; after the
later one, ``\ldots now?'' and, for a calendar day between the two sessions,
``What was my \emph{attribute} on \emph{day}?'' --- omitted for the two pairs
whose sessions share a day. Its gold answer is the earlier value, which assumes
that value held until the later session; LongMemEval does not say when a change
took effect, so a revision made before it was mentioned would make that gold
wrong. Scoring is the grid's: an answer is exact if it
states the expected value on token boundaries and does not state the
superseded one. The first question is asked before the revision exists, so it
has nothing to filter and serves as a control; the full conversation itself
passes it in 46 of 62 cases. The 16 it fails are pairs whose earlier session
already mentions the later value incidentally (``here are five tips'');
excluding them changes none of the comparisons below.

\textbf{The grid's two failure modes reappear.} At valid time the retaining
stores, read at the retrieval layer, return the superseded value in 41 to 60 of

appears in fewer than half of their answers (21 and 23): mostly, it is gone. Read by an

assumption that the gold itself makes.

store that consolidated every revision would not. This is consistent with,
though not traced to, the dropped \texttt{UPDATE} actions reported in
\S\ref{sec:external}. No configuration answers more than 35 of 62.

These are 62 cases on one backbone, so we read them as a check of direction
rather than as rates; every answer is in the released transcripts.

\subsection{Two bounds the measurements obey}\label{sec:bounds}

The two failure modes are not only observed; each follows from what its design
keeps, and the history alone fixes how far it can go.

\textbf{Consolidation has a ceiling.} A consolidating store replaces a value
when a newer one supersedes it, keeps it when it merely expires, and drops it
when it is purged. A store that does this for every revision holds at most the
latest value per subject that was neither superseded nor purged, so it cannot
answer a valid-time question whose answer is any other value, and the share it
could answer is computable from the history before the system runs. On the main
grid that ceiling is 0.385 (75 of 195): Mem0 reaches 0.364 on
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and 0.374 on \texttt{gpt-4o}, LangMem 0.323, and none
answers a single question above it. In the scale sweep the ceiling is 0.250 at
8, 32 and 128 entities alike, and Mem0's 0.206, 0.211 and 0.195 sit under it,
with 13 of its 915 correct answers above it. Each of the 13 states a superseded
value Mem0 still held when asked --- an update it did not apply --- so they mark
where Mem0 failed to consolidate, not answers the bound forbids. Mem0's flat valid-time accuracy
across scale (\S\ref{sec:scale}) is this ceiling, which the four-revision
workload fixes. We did not get the bound right first: counting only values still
valid at query time, it was exceeded by 9 Mem0 and 7 LangMem answers, every one
about an expired value, which is how we learned that expiry does not overwrite.
Because the corrected form was fixed after seeing those exceptions, its fit on
the main grid is post hoc; the scale runs, scored only afterwards, are its
held-out test.

\textbf{Retaining stores answer what needs no filtering.} Split each question by
whether a superseded value is known when it is asked. Read at the retrieval
layer, the three retaining stores answer 0.972 to 1.000 of the questions with
nothing to filter and at most 0.009 of the rest --- Graphiti on both backbones
and at every scale, Cognee and Hindsight on the main grid. Their accuracy is therefore the share of questions with nothing to
filter: 0.250 for Graphiti at every scale, and 0.408 on the main grid's
transaction-scoped questions, which Cognee and Hindsight score exactly and
Graphiti with one answer more (0.412). We noticed this from the scale result,
so on synthetic histories it describes rather than predicts. Its out-of-sample
test is the benchmark conversations, and there it partly fails: of the 122
questions with something to filter, Cognee answers 0 and Hindsight 9, but
Graphiti 18 (0.148 [0.095, 0.221]), above every synthetic interval. How often a retaining
store happens to filter is not a fixed property of the store; we suspect,
without having tested it, that real sessions crowd the old value out of the top
five with unrelated facts.

\subsection{What a retroactive correction does}\label{sec:corrections}

Every history so far was recorded in valid-time order. A retroactive correction
--- ``Elif Tanaka's home city from 2 January until 4 February was actually
Millholm'',
recorded weeks later --- is the case that pulls the two time axes apart, so we
added it to the generator and ran Mem0 and Graphiti on 80 subjects, 45 of which
receive one and the rest serve as the control. The three predictions and the
analysis were committed and published before the runs existed.

\textbf{A consolidating store applies a correction about the past to the
present.} Asked for the current value once the correction is recorded, Mem0
answers 35 of 35 subjects without a correction and 1 of 45 with one; we
predicted a drop of at least 0.10 and measured 0.978. Reading the answers
afterwards, for 41 of the 45 the current value appears in no answer about that
subject at all: the store has replaced it with the corrected past value. The
corrected interval itself Mem0 answers 69 times in 78, against 0 of 70 for
ordinary superseded intervals, also as predicted. Consolidation keeps one
statement per subject, and the newest statement wins whatever time it is about.

\textbf{A retaining store keeps both and filters neither.} Graphiti answers the
corrected interval 1 time in 78, within the predicted 0.10: it returns the
replaced value beside the correction. Forward replay asks each question at its
own checkpoint, so whether a store can still say what it believed \emph{before}
a correction is not tested here; none of the systems exposes that axis
(Table~\ref{tab:fragment}, C2).

\subsection{A defect that moves Mem0's scores}\label{sec:external}

Mem0 silently dropped memory \texttt{UPDATE}/\texttt{DELETE} actions
during ingestion in a LongMemEval pilot --- 15 across 60 conversations, and 23
across 6 conversations of the longer split, counted from the library's error log
during those runs, which the sealed artifacts do not keep --- which we traced to an unvalidated
positional-id lookup swallowed by a broad exception handler while
\texttt{add()} reports success. Dropped updates leave superseded values in
place, which lowers Mem0's current-state scores and can raise its valid-time
ones (the 13 answers above the ceiling in \S\ref{sec:bounds} are of this kind),
so the defect does not bias Mem0's aggregate in one known direction.

\section{Negative Results and Corrections}\label{sec:negative}

Results of the harness that concern only our reference implementation ---
retrieval-channel ablations, confidence decay, fusion calibration, signed
provenance --- are preserved with their negative outcomes in the artifact and not
developed here. Within the thesis, \textbf{four preregistered predictions
failed} and are reported where they were tested: that Mem0's current release
would still consolidate (\S\ref{sec:releases}); that a common reader would
recover at least 0.80 of valid time from the retaining stores, and that LangMem
read the same way would stay under its ceiling (\S\ref{sec:reader}); and that a
plain reader would recover valid time from Cognee's dated chunks of benchmark
text (\S\ref{sec:realdata}).

\textbf{Two of our own claims did not survive this revision.} We had reported
that Hindsight's reader recovers valid time far less often than Cognee's; it
recovers it equally and states more (\S\ref{sec:reader}). And our purge
category asked for less than our request did: the generator's typed purge
removes only the latest value, while the request asks for everything about the
attribute, so 29 of its 45 questions expected a value the request had asked to
delete; we now report it under both readings.

Three measurements were \textbf{retracted rather than quietly fixed}, and a
fourth claim was narrowed. Graphiti cell group ids repeated across runs while
the Neo4j reset did not wipe the group, so later runs queried graphs still
holding earlier episodes (36 per cell where one run produces 12); the affected
filtered and extractor runs are marked invalid and were re-run under verified
isolation. A distractor-pressure comparison set three instances against a
ten-instance mean and reversed under matching, so it was withdrawn rather than
reported. And a first purge scan reported Mem0 at zero residual because it read
Graphiti's raw database but only Mem0's
API-exposed memories; the corrected scan adds Mem0's vector store and history
database with a per-run database path.

\textbf{Two harness defects were caught before citation.} A revoked API key
made every call of two Hindsight runs fail, and the harness scored the empty
answers as abstentions; it now stops on authentication errors. And Cognee read
its storage location from a setting under a different name than the one we set,
so all Cognee runs shared one metadata database. An earlier version of this
paper reported a \textsc{chunks} run that logged 40 unexplained worker errors at
0.207 overall; the replication with isolated stores that we now cite scores
0.194 (\S\ref{sec:reader}).

\textbf{A first scale sweep was withdrawn.} Its relabelling ran out of natural
names and values and silently fell back to synthetic tokens as the store grew
--- 19\%, 55\% and 66\% of values at 8, 32 and 128 entities --- and values were
drawn with replacement, so people increasingly shared them. Graphiti appeared
to \emph{improve} at 128 entities, with exact accuracy rising from 0.117 to
0.216: answers that lost the queried value lost the superseded one too, and an
abstention question scores the absence of a superseded value as correct. The
controlled sweep of \S\ref{sec:scale} replays the same seeded histories with
only value identity changed, and at 32 entities Graphiti's recall moves from
0.705 to 0.977. Two lessons generalise. A scale experiment on LLM-backed memory
must hold token naturalness fixed, since it moves recall more than scale does;
and an exact-match metric rewards an empty answer on abstention questions, so
recall must be reported beside it.

\section{Reproducibility}

All artifacts are at \url{https://github.com/suanlab/temvera}, with a frozen
snapshot attached to its submission release. Every figure this paper prints is
paired with a recomputation from a sealed run, and one command checks them all
offline, with no API key, database server or dataset download --- except the
two dropped-update counts of \S\ref{sec:external}, which come from run logs.
Runs are sealed
with canonical config, environment, source-tree hash and per-file checksums, and
verified by an aggregate checker. The frozen synthetic
fixture is byte-reproducible and split-disjoint. Tests and lint pass on CPython
3.11. The LongMemEval corpus itself is downloaded rather than redistributed.
Two kinds of sealed output do carry its text, which the MIT licence permits and
which cannot be removed without breaking the runs' checksums: the
\runeseven{} run's history database of extracted memories, and the benchmark
runs' transcripts, which record retrieved passages verbatim. The labels we
derived are released; the 7 excluded pairs are listed by question ID, with
their reasons.

\section*{Limitations}

The grid's histories are synthetic with hand-specified operation profiles, so
contradiction density is approximated by operation mix and revision count rather
than sampled from real usage; the benchmark check covers 62 of 69 candidate
supersessions, 19 of them labelled by the author from AI-proposed labels. Five external systems in seven configurations
run on \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and were re-run on \texttt{gpt-4o}, one provider's
models only; LLM extraction is not bit-reproducible, LangMem and Hindsight do not
run at temperature~0, and Hindsight's reader is not stable across runs. The
first same-protocol replication moved a small-denominator category by 0.125,
which is why we draw no conclusion from expiry. The deletion
result rests on a single seeded history, so it establishes a direction, not a
rate. The literature review is single-reviewer. Purge claims cover declared
stores and tested failure points, not backups, OS caches or provider-side
copies.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work evaluates five open-source memory systems on synthetic histories and
on a public benchmark, and reports defects we found in them. We
report an implementation defect in one system that causes silent loss of memory
updates; it moves that system's scores in both directions, and we report it
beside them rather than correct for it. No user data was collected. The public benchmark is used locally under its
own terms; the split we use is MIT-licensed, which we verified against the
dataset's published metadata rather than assuming. No human subjects participated: a protocol for a
human audit study is released with the artifact but was not run, and we make no
human-effectiveness claim. Finally, the deletion result has a user-facing
consequence we want stated plainly --- an assistant asked in conversation to
forget something may not do so --- which is a privacy risk for deployments that
treat conversational deletion requests as effective.

\section*{Future Work}

Whether provenance-linked diffs actually help human
auditors is the largest untested claim. A preregisterable protocol, a seeded
planted-fault generator, a crossover assignment and a deterministic scorer are
included in the artifact, but no participants were recruited, so we make no
human-effectiveness claim. Running it requires ethics review or a documented
exemption, a pilot for variance, and an a priori power analysis.
