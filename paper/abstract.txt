Long-running agents accumulate facts that change, expire, and must sometimes be deleted. Answering what was true then, and what the agent knew then, requires separating valid time from transaction time and honouring deletion across every store a query can reach. Agent memory systems now advertise these capabilities; we measure whether they deliver them.

We replay deterministic lifecycle histories, rendered as natural language and scored against an oracle cross-checked by an independent SQLite implementation, into five open-source systems — Mem0, LangMem, Graphiti, Cognee and Hindsight — under forward-checkpoint transaction-time replay. Two designs emerge. Consolidating stores overwrite superseded values, so they answer current queries well (0.952 and 0.890) but historical ones poorly (0.364 and 0.323). Retaining stores keep the history but do not filter it: read at the retrieval layer, three independent implementations score between 0.000 and 0.015 on valid-time queries, a deficit that also depends on the retrieval budget. An LLM reading the same stores shows the history was there — Cognee's reader answers 192 of 195 valid-time cases — but cannot be relied on to use it: Hindsight's answers 67. Deletion divides the same way: native deletion APIs clear retrieval-reachable stores, while deletion requested in prose is silently ignored. Cost is write-dominated, and where it falls is a design choice.

We claim no novelty for bitemporal modelling or forgetting evaluation. The contribution is measurement, and the finding that a system can expose temporal fields, and even filter on them, without answering temporal queries correctly.
