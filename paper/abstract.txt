Long-running agents accumulate facts that change, expire, and must sometimes be deleted. Answering what was true then, and what the agent knew then, requires separating valid time from transaction time and honouring deletion in every store. Agent memory systems advertise both; we measure whether they deliver them.

We replay deterministic lifecycle histories, scored by an oracle that a second implementation checks, into five open-source systems — Mem0, LangMem, Graphiti, Cognee and Hindsight — and commit the predictions of every follow-up experiment before running it. Two designs emerge. Consolidating stores overwrite superseded values: they answer current queries well (0.952 and 0.890) and historical ones under a ceiling the history fixes (0.364 and 0.323 against 0.385); read in full, they keep 0 and 7 of 75 superseded values; and Mem0 applies a correction about the past to the present (1 of 45 current values answered afterwards). Retaining stores keep the history but do not filter it: at the retrieval layer three implementations answer no valid-time question, while a common reader recovers 0.73 to 0.77 of them, and changing only the retention policy under a fixed retriever and reader reproduces the split. Once a correction is stored, only a reader over dated source text can say what was known before it (Cognee, 41 of 45). Deletion divides too: three systems ignore prose requests, Mem0 honours one wording of three and LangMem part of each; native APIs clear every answer when given the right records but leave copies in stores no query reads, and deleting what a search returns erases up to 28 of 36 people never named. The findings survive a second backbone, replicate runs, current releases (where Mem0 has switched designs) and 62 supersessions in LongMemEval's conversations.

We claim no novelty for bitemporal modelling or forgetting evaluation: the contribution is measurement.
