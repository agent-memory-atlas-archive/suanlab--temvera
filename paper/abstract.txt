Long-running agents accumulate facts that change, expire, and must sometimes be deleted. Answering what was true then, and what the agent knew then, needs valid time and transaction time, and deletion must reach every store. Agent memory systems advertise both; we ask where in their pipelines it holds.

We replay deterministic lifecycle histories, scored by an oracle that a second implementation checks, into five open-source systems — Mem0, LangMem, Graphiti, Cognee and Hindsight — and measure separately what a store retains, what its retrieval selects for the asked time, what an answer asserts, and what deletion removes. The predictions of every follow-up experiment were committed before it ran; sixteen failed, and we report them. The layers disagree. Stores that overwrite superseded values keep almost none of them and answer historical questions only up to a ceiling the history fixes (Mem0: 0.364 against 0.385), and Mem0 applies a correction about the past to the present. Stores that keep the history answer no valid-time question at their retrieval layer, yet one common reader answers 0.73 to 0.77 of them from the same text where it carries dates, and changing only the retention policy under a fixed retriever and reader reproduces the split. Once a correction is stored, only the systems' own readers say what was known before it. Three systems ignore prose deletion requests; native APIs clear every answer but leave copies in stores no query reads, and a caller that deletes what a search returns, unchecked, erases up to 28 of 36 people it was never asked about.

We claim no novelty for bitemporal modelling or forgetting evaluation: the contribution is a measurement contract and what it shows.
