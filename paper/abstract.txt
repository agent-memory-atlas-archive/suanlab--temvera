Long-running agents accumulate facts that change, expire, and must sometimes be deleted. Answering what was true then, and what the agent knew then, requires separating valid time from transaction time and honouring deletion across every store a query can reach. Agent memory systems now advertise these capabilities; we measure whether they deliver them.

We replay deterministic lifecycle histories, scored against an independently cross-checked oracle, into five open-source systems — Mem0, LangMem, Graphiti, Cognee and Hindsight — under forward-checkpoint transaction-time replay. Two designs emerge. Consolidating stores overwrite superseded values, so they answer current queries well (0.952 and 0.890) but historical ones poorly (0.364 and 0.323), just under a ceiling (0.385) that the history alone fixes, and Mem0 applies a correction about the past to the present (1 of 45 current values answered afterwards). Retaining stores keep the history but do not filter it: read at the retrieval layer, three independent implementations score 0.000 on valid-time queries, yet one deterministic read-path rule recovers up to 190 of 195 from the dates they return. An LLM reader recovers it for Cognee (192 of 195) but not for Hindsight (67). Mem0's current release switches to the second design, and its accuracy follows. In Mem0 and Graphiti, deletion divides the same way: native deletion APIs clear retrieval-reachable stores, while deletion requested in prose is silently ignored. Both failure modes hold from 8 to 128 people per history and on 62 supersessions in LongMemEval's annotator-edited conversations. Cost is write-dominated in Mem0 and read-dominated in Cognee: where it falls is a design choice.

We claim no novelty for bitemporal modelling or forgetting evaluation: the contribution is measurement, and the finding that a system can expose temporal fields without answering temporal queries correctly.
